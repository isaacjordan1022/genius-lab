# Curriculum and assessment proposal

Status: proposed implementation specification for alignment. The 90% progression gate is a settled requirement; quantities, schedules, and rubrics below are product decisions to validate with the family, not research-established cutoffs.

## Structure and realistic coverage

A **weekly lesson** contains independent objectives across selected subjects, five daily chunks, and a weekly test. A **chunk** teaches first and then presents exactly 15 scored items. A **session** is one visit: a child may finish a chunk over multiple sittings. Save the current activity, draft response, item state, assistance, and completed attempts automatically. Returning should offer “Pick up where you left off.”

Plan 15–20 minutes per chunk, or 75–100 minutes across five chunks, plus a proposed 15–20-minute weekly test. These are estimates, not timers or guaranteed maximums. Fifteen scored items alongside substantive teaching is ambitious, especially for Ezra. Use short spoken responses and visual interactions; split his chunk into two sittings when necessary. Never rush an answer to satisfy the advertised duration. If trials consistently exceed the budget, revisit item count with the parent rather than quietly removing teaching or reducing challenge.

Every week retains language/comprehension, new vocabulary, mathematics, age-appropriate Torah, and one featured artist. Rotate the deeper investigation across science, geography, music, history, and Jewish history; brief exposures can connect these fields without claiming each has received substantial instruction. Mishnah, Hilchos Shabbos, and Gemara are possible Torah strands, selected for readiness and supplied material. Publish a four-week coverage map so rotation is intentional. A decorative theme can unify presentation without forcing unrelated objectives into one story. **Alignment/pilot question:** can this breadth, meaningful teaching, and 15 scored items fit 15–20 minutes for each child? Preserve the requested subjects while testing the schedule; any scope or duration adjustment needs explicit agreement rather than silent omission.

## Example weekly objective matrix

This example includes the weekly core, one artist, and geography as the rotating focus. Music, science, and history can receive brief introductions here and deeper attention in later weeks. Quantities describe the intended evidence, not claims of lasting mastery.

Grade informs the starting proposal; onboarding and parent knowledge establish actual readiness. Do not interpret a single success as placement evidence. Ezra receives audio, pictures, and spoken answers; keyboard speed and reading ability must not accidentally become prerequisites for assessing other knowledge.

## Five chunks and weekly test

Each chunk follows: brief retrieval warm-up → explicit teaching with worked examples → guided practice → 15 scored items → explanation/correction → progress result. An optional short, fixed-duration arcade interlude can sit between teaching and assessment; it earns no assessment credit. Daily missions remain distinct activities after the lesson chunk.

| Chunk | Teaching emphasis | Distribution of 15 scored items |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Story evidence, first vocabulary, selected mathematics skill | 5 comprehension; 5 language; 5 mathematics |
| 2 | Featured artist, Torah source, literary comparison | 5 art; 5 Torah; 5 language |
| 3 | Map evidence, mathematical application, Torah return | 5 geography; 6 mathematics; 4 Torah |
| 4 | Story construction and purposeful artistic choices | 5 comprehension/storytelling; 5 art; 5 language |
| 5 | New applications and spaced return to earlier teaching | 3 comprehension; 3 mathematics; 3 geography; 3 Torah; 3 art |

Starting item mix: eight multiple-choice, four matching, three short open responses per chunk. Matching is one scored item even when it contains several pairs; its credit rule must be explicit. Multiple-choice can assess inference and application, not merely fact recall. At least two open responses should require reasoning or creative application. Short responses may be spoken, selected-and-explained, or written; substantial essays cannot fit this schedule.

The weekly test proposes 15 fresh items: three comprehension/storytelling, three vocabulary/language, three mathematics, two Torah, two art, and two rotating-focus items. Its pass threshold is 54/60. Provide no teaching or correctness feedback until submission; teach and correct afterward. In later weeks, allocate five items to previously taught objectives and ten to the current week, while maintaining strand balance across tests. These delayed checks measure what remains available later; show their results separately. A passed current-week test alone is not evidence of long-term retention.

## Scoring and the strict 90% gate

Every item carries four possible rubric points: 60 points total; **54/60 unlocks progression**. Use the unrounded total for gating. Show percentage plus points. MC items receive 0 or 4; matching receives 0–4 under its predeclared rule, with four pairs as a simple one-point-per-correct-pair format. All open responses use the 0–4 rubric below.

Passing a chunk unlocks the next chunk, that day's open play, and the specified power-up award. Open play never advances curriculum. Passing all five chunks unlocks the weekly test; passing that test at 90% unlocks the next weekly lesson. There is no completion bypass, time-served bypass, or easier-question route around the gate.

Below 90%: explain missed concepts, give guided practice, then offer a fresh, equivalent 15-item retry against the same objectives and difficulty blueprint. Preserve **first independent attempt**, **every retry**, and **first passing attempt** separately. Never assemble a passing score from the best answers across attempts. Assisted practice does not enter the gate score. A hint requested during scored work moves that attempt to practice; explain this before granting it.

Permit stopping and resuming without penalty. Persistent difficulty prompts parent support and further teaching; progression stays locked. Invalid questions are replaced by equivalent reviewed questions and the affected attempt recalculated with an audit trail. This corrects assessment errors without granting a discretionary bypass. Retries cannot repeatedly earn the same ticket or power-up award.

## Open responses, creativity, and reliable grading

Use task-specific rubrics with **four declared one-point criteria**, each with observable meets/does-not-yet-meet examples. An inference task might award one point each for a relevant interpretation, a correct source detail, a logical connection between them, and addressing the requested comparison. A creative task might award points for meeting the prompt, including a specified element, making a coherent purposeful choice, and explaining its effect. Criteria must suit the child's age and the actual prompt; avoid mechanically demanding four sentences or adult prose from Ezra. Total 0–4 is the count of criteria demonstrated. A creative response need not resemble the model answer. Multiple interpretations are valid when supported. Score mechanics only when grammar/spelling is the explicit objective. Use formative feedback for other errors.

Each generated item must carry its objective, source, expected reasoning, acceptable alternatives, scoring anchors, and difficulty designation. Exact-answer tasks use deterministic checks where possible. Open responses receive AI-proposed rubric scores with reviewable rationale; ambiguous grades need adjudication before a gating decision. Neither a second model nor self-reported confidence proves correctness.

Torah lessons use backend-curated parent material; V1 has no upload interface. Preserve source/page references, confirm Hebrew/Aramaic extraction, and distinguish text, translation, commentary, and interpretation. Unclear source material requires review before publication. Use an original playful, slightly mischievous narrator who jokes about the adventure, never the child's ability or mistakes.

## Evidence and reporting

Retrieval and spaced checks are supported by primary research: [Karpicke and Bauernschmidt](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21574747/) found retention benefits from spacing repeated retrieval; [Rohrer and colleagues](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20053059/) tested retrieval and transfer with fourth- and fifth-grade students. These studies do not establish our timing, rubric, 90% gate, or age-four format.

Parent reports distinguish initial independent performance, improvement after teaching, eventual passing score, and delayed/new-application evidence. Report counts and examples, such as “first attempt 42/60; retry 56/60; seven-day check 3/4 items independent,” rather than a single opaque mastery percentage. Flag sparse evidence and disputed grades. Three children's progress can guide this product, but cannot establish that the app alone caused gains over school and other learning.
